Context: From B2B to B2C

After two years in B2B textile marketing (Ajmera Fashion, Jalan Mega Mart), Swad Cooking Institute was a deliberate shift into B2C. Swad's business is selling cooking and baking courses — offline masterclasses, online video programs, and membership subscriptions.

I joined as Social Media Manager. As someone genuinely passionate about food and cooking, I could evaluate content quality and culinary credibility quickly — which turned out to matter more than expected, since it shaped which chefs, recipes, and formats I chose to push.

The Course Catalogue

  • Offline Classes: Physical masterclasses and hobby/diploma batches with 100% hands-on training, including knife and cutting technique. The Basic Cooking offline course runs 10–12 days at roughly ₹10,000.
  • Online Classes: Pre-recorded video lessons, detailed recipe PDFs, and WhatsApp voice-message support for kitchen queries.
  • Key Topics: Bakery & Confectionery, Street Food & Chaat, Regional Indian Thalis (Punjabi, Rajasthani, Maharashtrian), International Cuisines, Fast Food, and Food Business Startup training.

Pricing & Membership Structure

  • Free Workshops: Regular introductory or festival-special sessions (Mango Mania Baking, Ice Cream & Sandwich workshops)
  • Individual/Specialty Courses: $4.99–$61.99 depending on depth
  • SWAD Super Save 1-Year Membership: ~₹3,499 / $37.99 — access to 160–170+ courses, 2,000+ recipes, and completion certificates
  • Lifetime Membership: ~₹44,999 for lifetime access to standard courses

CASE 01

The Flagship Diploma Course — Turning Students Into Marketing Assets

The One-Year Diploma in Culinary Arts & Bakery was Swad's highest-ticket course — six months of hands-on training plus a six-month internship. It was the institute's biggest revenue driver and its strongest credibility signal with 5-star hotel recruiters and competitors alike.

I noticed most diploma enrollments originated from Instagram and YouTube, so I built a strategy specifically to grow that funnel: partnering with chefs to run live sessions featuring current students.

The logic: giving students a public platform to showcase their skill built their confidence — and confident students shared their own livestream appearances, turning them into organic promoters. Prospective viewers got to see exactly how the course worked and left motivated to pursue a restaurant career themselves.

Result: higher views, stronger engagement, new-viewer growth, more qualified leads for the sales team, and free PR for both chefs and students — a self-reinforcing loop of enrollment revenue and organic marketing.

CASE 03

Matching Content to Chef Face-Value

Spending more time embedded with the chef team surfaced a pattern: certain chefs carried more on-camera "face value" for certain topics. Chef Sneha, the institute's owner, consistently drove higher views when she taught authentic, traditional recipes or shared cooking tips — more than on other topic types.

I ran the same analysis across the rest of the chef roster and matched each of them to the topics and recipes that played to their individual strengths. I also pushed topics beyond straight recipes, which drew a noticeably more positive comment section.

The sharpest insight: everyday topics that viewers assume they already know — but told with one new fact or method — consistently outperformed novel recipes. A simple video on making ghee at home, reframed around an easier method, became the seed for a full "How to Store" series:

  • How to Store / Freeze Mangoes, How to Store Mango Pulp Long-Term — 359K views
  • Free Dry Masala Workshop, non-preservative Dry Masala at home — 233K views
  • Storage Tricks: Green Chilli Paste, Lemon Juice, Green Peas — 208K views
  • How to Store Green Leaves & Mint Leaves Longer — 133K views
  • How to Select Safe & Smart Kitchen Tools (Best & Worst Cookware Materials) — 115K views

CASE 11

The Revenue Insight the Company Didn't Act On

After months of steady livestream output, one problem remained unresolved: YouTube ad revenue wasn't scaling with watch hours. I introduced YouTube Membership as a new revenue lever — but the gap between total watch hours and estimated revenue stayed disproportionate.

YouTube Membership Page →

The diagnosis: daily livestreams drew strong same-day views and engagement, but became functionally "dead" afterward — almost nobody rewatches a 50-minute live recording cluttered with off-topic chat and unrelated discussion once the live moment has passed.

My recommendation: reduce raw livestream output and instead upload tightly edited recipe videos. Edited uploads attract new viewers, generate repeat views, and produce more durable revenue — while doing fewer lives actually increases the credibility and appointment-value of the lives that remain.

Example: Dry Kachori / Diwali Farsan Edited Upload →

The strategy began working, but leadership reversed course and paused the edited-upload format before it had time to mature. My assessment: any new content algorithm strategy needs a minimum six-month runway to properly train the platform and the audience — reverting early undercut the results before they could compound.

I also proposed shooting livestreams and standalone videos simultaneously to capture both formats from a single session — this wasn't implemented before I left.

Key Learnings

1. Familiar Topics Beat Novel Ones — With the Right Twist

Viewers don't need a new recipe. They need one new fact about something they think they already know. The entire "How to Store" series and Monday Mistake series were built on this single insight.

2. Talent Has a Topic-Fit, Not Just a Follower Count

Matching each chef's face-value to the right topic type outperformed treating all chefs as interchangeable hosts.

3. Watch Hours ≠ Revenue Without a Content-Format Strategy

Livestreams generate real-time engagement but poor long-tail value. Edited uploads compound. A channel needs both — and the mix has to be deliberate.

4. New Strategies Need Minimum Runway

Reversing a working strategy before six months undercuts it before the platform algorithm and audience habits can mature around it.

5. Constraints Can Become Content

Lost footage became three successful reels rather than a wasted shoot — creative recovery is a skill, not luck.

6. People Remember How You Leave

The farewell Diwali marathon wasn't a growth tactic. It was five and a half hours of gratitude to a team and audience. That instinct — to close well, not just perform well — is part of how I work.
